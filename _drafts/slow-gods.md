Is [science fiction played out]? If so, maybe the answer is cross-pollination. To that end, [_Slow Gods_][2] has been described as philosophical science-fiction. The opening line, "My name is Mawukana na-Vdnaze, and I am a very poor copy of myself," sets the stage for some big questions. The book's cosmic horror conception of faster-than-light travel is a pleasing Lovecraftian twist. Arcspace, or just _the dark_ drives starship pilots mad and seems to be a  malevolent presence in its own right with an unhealthy curiousity about the living.

[Claire North][1] is a pseudonym of Catherine Webb who also writes under the name Kate Griffin. Both of her parents are authors. Her father, [Nick Webb][3], was also a publisher and in that capacity signed Douglass Adams to write the book version of _Hitchhiker's Guide to the Galaxy_.

The book posits the existance of many genders with an array of pronouns - qe, qis, xe, te, ter, etc. I thought the surplus of genders would be annoying but it quickly becomes invisible. I suppose it allows the reader latitude to imagine the romantic interest as they wish? Curiously, the protagonist along with the main villian were both standard-issue male. So, we see relation to other's gender without mention what those other genders would be like internally, let alone mechanically. One imagines one of those international adaptors with prongs and sockets of every description. How on earth does one plug one's iPhone in?

The Shine is a hyper-capitalist dystopia which felt very familiar, very MAGA America. Adjumir has European vibes - refined, cultured, and humane. The collective hivemind, the Consensus, might map to China or East Asia. Downtrodden Nitashi stands in for the Global South. The plot tracks real-world headlines maybe a bit too closely. Search and replace ‘climate change’ for ‘supernova’ and ‘MAGA’ for ‘the Shine’ and there you have it.

One discrepency detracted a little. A supernova is an event beyond human control so there's not the same culpabilty. The inconvenient fact is that we, consumers and citizens, are complicit in the degradation of our Earthly environment. We are accountable not just our handling or mishandling climate change but for causing it.

The longer this book sits in my head, the more I keep revising my opinion upward. She's making a deep point. In the long long run, what is "good" becomes almost mathematical, but distant, bloodless, alien, and abstract from the mammalian concerns of human beings. ...something we can maybe glimpse intellectually, while remaining viscerally trapped in our animal lust, rage, and fear. Maw, the central character, becomes disconnected from the primal therefore is no longer quite human and can't figure out exactly what he has become. The question then is between an expansive universal good on a time scale of millennia or the individual connections and partialities that give life meaning. Can we have one without giving up the other?

In the long run, the arc of history bends toward the light, but slowly. In the meantime, you've got to be at home in the dark.

[Haydn on Goodreads][5] sums it up nicely: “In the end, the ‘slow gods’ were really the friends we made along the way: death, grief, the struggle for meaning.”
